# Known limitations

What this system cannot do, ordered by how much it matters. Every item here is either measured
in [TEST_CASES.md](TEST_CASES.md) or falls straight out of the architecture.

---

## Critical

### L1 · The interactions are simulated

No public dataset of real YouTube watch histories exists ([DATASET.md](DATASET.md)). Every
number in this repo describes behaviour generated by
[`simulator.py`](../src/recsys/data/simulator.py).

**What that does not undermine:** the algorithms. The same `ImplicitALS` and `CoVisitation`
classes beat popularity by 1.9× on MovieLens-100k — 100,000 ratings from real people.

**What it does undermine:** any claim about real-world performance. There is none in this repo,
deliberately.

### L2 · A single-interest viewer gets a monoculture

Five Gaming videos in → a 100% Gaming page out (1 of 13 categories). Measured as
[F7](TEST_CASES.md#f7--filter-bubble--a-single-interest-history-yields-a-monoculture).

The mechanism is diagnosed, not guessed: cross-category candidates *are* retrieved (trending
alone contributes 13 categories), but they score low, and the exploration slots' global
relevance floor filters exactly those candidates out. MMR can only diversify among what
survives ranking.

This is the difference between a recommender and an echo chamber, and it is the first thing I
would fix.

### L3 · Offline metrics cannot validate this system

Demonstrated with an oracle control: the true generative model *also* loses to a popularity
baseline on full-catalog retrieval ([EVALUATION.md](EVALUATION.md)). Users can only click what
they were shown, so logged data measures the previous policy as much as the new one.

The counterfactual protocols are better but still not proof. **Only an online A/B test can
settle whether this system is good**, and nothing here substitutes for that.

## Significant

### L4 · Cold items are near-invisible to two of three recall sources

2,282 of 6,000 videos (38%) have no co-visitation neighbours; a video with zero clicks has an
ALS factor of exactly zero. Only content similarity and trending can surface it
([F1](TEST_CASES.md#f1--cold-items--the-structural-hole-in-collaborative-filtering)).

Inherent to collaborative filtering, mitigated by the hybrid, not solved. On a platform
receiving 500 hours of upload per minute this is a first-order problem.

### L5 · Search matches title format as well as topic

*"beginner guide to investing in index funds"* returns *"A Beginner Guide to GPU"* at rank 1
([F4](TEST_CASES.md#f4--the-tf-idf-template-trap)). TF-IDF cannot distinguish a common title
template from subject matter. Neural embeddings would largely fix it and are already supported
as a config flag; they are not the default because of image size.

### L6 · Multi-interest users get a diluted profile

The content profile is a weighted mean, so a user who likes cooking *and* Formula 1 gets a
centroid matching neither ([F3](TEST_CASES.md#f3--contradictory-history--6-unrelated-categories)).
Partially mitigated by a recency anchor and a max-over-history feature; the real fix is
clustering the history into several profiles and retrieving for each.

### L7 · Watch time over-rewards long-form content

`log_duration_min` is a top-3 ranker feature. Watch time does not distinguish *engaged* from
*unable to stop*. YouTube's own answer — survey-based "valued watch time" — needs a signal we do
not have.

### L8 · No sequence modelling

The user is a bag of watched items with recency decay. Real sessions have *order* and *intent*:
watching a trailer then a review then a tutorial is a trajectory, not a set. A GRU4Rec or
SASRec-style sequential model would capture this.

## Moderate

### L9 · No cross-device or long-term personalisation

History lives in the browser and dies with it. Deliberate for a stateless demo
([D13](DESIGN_DECISIONS.md)); wrong for a product.

### L10 · Static catalog

Artifacts load once at startup; new uploads are invisible until the next build. Production needs
incremental indexing and periodic factor refresh.

### L11 · Brute-force retrieval will not scale past ~10⁶ items

6,000 items × 256 dims is sub-millisecond. At a million items an ANN index (FAISS/HNSW) becomes
necessary. The `RecallResult` interface is designed so this swap touches one class.

### L12 · ALS retraining is a full batch job

18 seconds here; hours at real scale. New *users* are handled by fold-in with no retraining, but
new *items* need a full refit.

### L13 · Pointwise ranking

Optimises per-item probability, not list order. LambdaMART would likely win on NDCG; we chose
pointwise because the watch-time weighting needs a calibrated score
([D7](DESIGN_DECISIONS.md)).

### L14 · Exploration is ε-greedy, not Thompson sampling

Reserved slots sampled by novelty explore uniformly rather than in proportion to *uncertainty*,
which is statistically wasteful.

---

## Minor / scope

### L15 · No abuse, safety, or quality filtering

Real YouTube runs borderline-content classifiers, spam detection, and authoritative-source
boosting for news and medical topics. This system has **none** — it would happily recommend
misinformation if the co-watch pattern suggested it. That is a serious gap for anything
user-facing, and out of scope here.

### L16 · No fairness or creator-equity constraints

Gini of exposure for the full pipeline is 0.809 — highly concentrated. Multi-stakeholder
recommendation (balancing viewer utility against creator exposure) is an active research area
and is not addressed.

### L17 · No A/B testing or online-learning infrastructure

No interleaving, no bandit feedback loop, no counterfactual policy evaluation beyond the offline
protocols already described.

### L18 · Synthetic titles are template-generated

The generator composes titles from 24 templates, which makes the template trap (L5) more
pronounced than it would be on real YouTube data. Run with `--source youtube_api` to remove this
artifact entirely.

### L19 · Feed order is randomised in the simulator

This makes position bias clean and unconfounded, but also makes our logs **less** biased than a
real production log would be. Real presentation bias is harder than what we simulate — so our
IPS correction is being evaluated on an easier problem than it would face in production.
